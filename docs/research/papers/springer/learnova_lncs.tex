\begin{abstract}
Learnova rebuilds the lecture decks that teachers already use into illustrated and interactive ones. Several of the things it
adds---worked solutions, small laboratories, diagrams and rewritten titles---could be produced by a language model, but a model
can make arithmetic errors, write unsafe code or drift from the slide. This paper describes the checks we designed so that
every added item is either computed, verified or replaced by an offline result. We present (i) a detector and a library of
exact solvers that turn a problem in slide text into a step-by-step explanation; (ii) a lab contract, a selection policy and a
static gate for model-written lab code; (iii) a whitelist sanitiser for model-drawn SVG diagrams with label fitting;
(iv) a lexical grounding filter for model-written titles and explanations; and (v) a paced key pool that keeps several
free-tier keys busy and skips spent ones. On a held-out set of 26 problems the solvers gave the correct answer every time,
with no false detections on 52 ordinary sentences. The sanitiser left no unsafe construct in 13 accepted diagrams out of 18
crafted answers, and the lab gate refused all 10 unsafe or broken programs after we fixed one gap the test exposed. With five
keys the pool produced 40 diagrams in 42\,s against 183\,s with one key, within 15\,s of a simple throughput bound, and
remembering spent keys cut the time to reach the offline fallback from 166\,s to 22\,s.
\keywords{worked examples \and step-by-step solutions \and interactive labs \and output validation \and SVG sanitising \and
rate-limited scheduling \and educational technology}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Most lecture decks in an engineering course are reused from year to year. They are dense, they show solved problems as
finished tables, and they rarely contain anything a student can try. Learnova takes such a deck and rebuilds it: every point of
the source is kept, and each slide gets a suitable visual form, a title that states its point, an illustration and, where it
helps, an explanation or a lab. The rebuilding itself is rule-based and described elsewhere. This paper is about the parts that
a language model could write, and about how we make them safe to put in front of a student.

The risk is real. A model asked to solve a problem may produce a confident wrong step~\cite{ji2023,kasneci2023}; a model asked
for a diagram may return markup with scripts or links; a model asked for a lab returns code that will run in a student's browser;
and a model asked to improve a title may write something unrelated to the slide~\cite{maynez2020}. In each case the cost of a bad
output is higher than the cost of no output, because students trust what appears on a course slide.

We therefore follow one rule throughout: \emph{a generated item is shown only if it passes a check that does not depend on the
model, and when it fails, an offline result takes its place}. Where the item can be computed exactly, as with arithmetic and the
dynamic programs taught in our courses, we do not ask the model at all.

A second constraint is cost. The college has no paid AI budget, so the system runs with free-tier keys, each limited to a few
requests per minute and a daily quota. A deck may need forty diagrams and ten labs, so how requests are spread over the keys
decides whether a deck takes one minute or five.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a problem detector with sixteen exact solvers that produce explained, step-by-step solutions (Sect.~\ref{sec:explain});
\item a lab contract, a lab selection policy and a static gate for model-written lab code, with an offline builder as the
fallback (Sect.~\ref{sec:labs});
\item a whitelist sanitiser for model-drawn SVG and a label-fitting rule (Sect.~\ref{sec:svg});
\item a lexical grounding filter for model-written titles and explanations (Sect.~\ref{sec:ground});
\item a paced key pool with a throughput bound that sets the number of parallel workers (Sect.~\ref{sec:pool});
\item an evaluation of each check on held-out and adversarial inputs, including two faults the evaluation found
(Sect.~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textit{Worked examples.} Novices learn more from studying solved problems than from solving the same problems unaided, as long as
each step is visible and its reason is stated~\cite{sweller1985,atkinson2000,renkl2014}. Computer algebra services such as
Wolfram$|$Alpha~\cite{wolframalpha} show steps for mathematics, and intelligent tutoring systems give step-level feedback that
approaches human tutoring~\cite{vanlehn2011}, but both need the problem in a clean form and neither reads lecture slides.

\textit{Interactive visualisation.} Meta-analyses of algorithm visualisation find that the learner's engagement, not the animation,
predicts learning~\cite{hundhausen2002,naps2002}; PhET shows that inquiry-style simulations work at scale~\cite{wieman2008}. The
ICAP framework~\cite{chi2014} and the wider evidence for active learning~\cite{freeman2014} support moving students from watching
to doing, and retrieval practice~\cite{roediger2006} motivates the ``predict, then reveal'' prompts of our labs.

\textit{Generated slides and their faithfulness.} Systems such as PPSGen~\cite{hu2015}, D2S~\cite{sun2021} and
DOC2PPT~\cite{fu2022} generate new slides from documents, and commercial tools~\cite{gamma,copilotppt,geminislides,canva} do the
same from a prompt. None of them checks what it generates. Studies of abstractive summarisation show that unchecked models add
unsupported content~\cite{maynez2020,ji2023}, which is the problem our checks address. Table~\ref{tab:related} places our work
against these lines.

\section{Checked Generation}
\label{sec:checked}
Figure~\ref{fig:flow} shows where the checks sit. For an input $x$ (a slide) and a kind of item $\kappa$ (explainer, lab,
diagram, title), let $M_\kappa(x)$ be the model's answer, $G_\kappa$ a check that does not use the model, and $F_\kappa(x)$ an
offline result that may be empty. The item shown is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:checked}
y_\kappa(x) =
\begin{cases}
S_\kappa(x) & \text{if an exact solver } S_\kappa \text{ applies},\\
M_\kappa(x) & \text{else if a key is available and } G_\kappa(M_\kappa(x)) = 1,\\
F_\kappa(x) & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The order matters. A computed answer is always preferred, because it is both correct and free. The model is used only where
nothing can be computed, and only through a check. The offline path guarantees that a deck is always finished, even with no
network at all.

\subsection{Escalating only on a weak offline result}
The college uses free-tier keys only, so every request has a cost in quota even when it has no cost in money. We therefore
compute the offline result first in every case and ask the model only when a test $W_\kappa$ says that result is weak:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:escalate}
\text{call } M_\kappa(x) \iff \text{mode}=\textsf{always} \;\vee\; \bigl(\text{mode}=\textsf{auto} \wedge W_\kappa(F_\kappa(x))=1\bigr),
\end{equation}
with a third mode, \textsf{off}, that never calls a model. Table~\ref{tab:weak} lists the tests. Each is cheap and does not use a
model: a readability test on Tesseract's output, a test of whether a title states a point, the figure kind a slide needs, and
the amount of structure available for an offline lab. Two places were already of this form before: a visual is refined by the
model only when the decision engine's confidence is below $0.7$, and an explainer is written by the model only when no exact
solver applies.

\section{Computed Step-by-Step Explainers}
\label{sec:explain}
\subsection{Representation}
An explainer and each of its steps are tuples
\begin{equation}
E = (\kappa, p, \langle s_1,\dots,s_n\rangle, a, \rho, \sigma), \qquad
s_i = (\mathit{title}_i, \mathit{work}_i, \mathit{why}_i, \mathit{rule}_i, T_i):
\end{equation}
the problem type, the statement, the steps, the answer, a short recap and the source
$\sigma \in \{\text{computed}, \text{slide}, \text{model}\}$. A step has a short action title, the lines of working
with the changed part marked, a plain reason, the general rule, and for table algorithms the state of the table $T_i$ with the
cells to highlight. The page that shows an explainer prints its source, so a student can see whether a number was computed.

\subsection{Detection}
Slide text rarely holds a clean problem. A system of equations may sit on two lines, a grammar may fill the slide, and a
problem may be one sentence in a paragraph. The detector builds candidates in a fixed order: the whole text, then each pair of
neighbouring lines that both contain an equals sign, then each line, then each sentence:
\begin{multline}
\mathit{Cand}(x) = \langle x \rangle \;\Vert\; \langle \ell_i ; \ell_{i+1} : \text{``=''} \in \ell_i, \ell_{i+1} \rangle \\
\;\Vert\; \langle \ell_i \rangle \;\Vert\; \langle \text{sentences of } \ell_i \rangle,
\end{multline}
with duplicates and strings longer than 4000 characters removed. Each candidate is offered to the solvers
$\mathcal{S} = \langle S_1, \dots, S_{16} \rangle$ in a fixed order, and the first explainer with at least three steps wins:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:first}
E^{*}(x) = \operatorname{first}\{\, S_j(c) : c \in \mathit{Cand}(x),\, j = 1..16,\, |S_j(c)| \ge 3 \,\}.
\end{equation}
The solver order puts the most specific patterns first (Viterbi, Naive Bayes, bigram, CYK, Bayes' rule) and the most general
last (percentages, statistics, speed), so a Bayes problem that contains a percentage is not taken by the percentage solver.

For algebra the equation is cut out of the sentence by removing leading words (``Solve'', ``Find $x$ if'', ``Solve for $y$
given'') and trailing clauses. The result is then rejected if either side still contains a word:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reject}
\mathit{reject}(q) \iff \exists\, w \in \mathit{lhs}(q) \cup \mathit{rhs}(q) : w \text{ matches } [A\text{--}Za\text{--}z]\{2,\}.
\end{equation}
This rule is deliberately strict. A sentence such as ``Version 2 of the system was released in 2026'' contains numbers but no
equation, and it is the strictness of Eq.~\ref{eq:reject} that keeps such sentences from being treated as problems.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Finding and solving a problem on a slide}
\label{alg:explain}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require slide text $x$, optional worked-example rows $W$ found by the visual engine
\For{$c \in \mathit{Cand}(x)$}
  \For{$S_j \in \mathcal{S}$ in order}
    \State $E \gets S_j(c)$ \Comment{exceptions count as no answer}
    \If{$E \ne \varnothing$ and $|E| \ge 3$} \Return $E$ with source ``computed'' \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\If{$W$ has $\ge 2$ rows and $\ge 60\%$ of rows have a reason or a known verb}
  \State \Return $E$ built from $W$ with source ``slide''
\EndIf
\If{a key is available and $x$ looks like a problem} \State \Return model explainer if it passes Eq.~\ref{eq:llm} \EndIf
\State \Return $\varnothing$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Solvers}
All arithmetic uses SymPy~\cite{meurer2017} or exact fractions, so an answer such as $3/28$ is shown as a fraction and as a
decimal and is never rounded in the middle of a calculation. Table~\ref{tab:solvers} lists the solvers and what their steps show.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{The sixteen solvers and the content of their steps}
\label{tab:solvers}
\begin{tabular}{p{3.6cm}p{8.0cm}}
\toprule
Solver & Steps \\
\midrule
Linear equation & write, expand, collect the unknown, remove the constant, divide, check by substitution \\
Quadratic equation & standard form, factor or formula, both roots, check \\
Pair of linear equations & elimination with the multiplier shown, back-substitution, check \\
Percentage, simple interest, speed & formula, substitution, result with units \\
Mean, median & sort or sum, count, divide or pick the middle \\
Probability, Bayes' rule & name the quantities, numerator, denominator, result \\
Pythagoras & formula, squares, sum, square root \\
Derivative, integral & rule applied term by term, then combined \\
CYK parsing & the chart cell by cell, with the split points and the rules that fire \\
Bigram probability & counts, each conditional probability, product \\
Naive Bayes classification & priors, smoothed likelihoods, scores, decision \\
Viterbi decoding & initialisation, recursion per time step, back-pointers, path \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Four solvers implement the dynamic programs of our course and are the reason we built exact solvers at all. For a bigram model
with sentence markers, the maximum-likelihood estimate~\cite{jurafsky2025} is computed for every pair of the query sentence:
\begin{equation}
P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{C(w_{i-1} w_i)}{C(w_{i-1})}, \qquad P(w_{1:n}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}).
\end{equation}
For Naive Bayes with classes $s$, vocabulary size $V$ and $N_s$ word tokens in class $s$, the solver shows the add-one estimate
and the decision:
\begin{equation}
\hat{s} = \arg\max_{s}\; P(s) \prod_{j} P(f_j \mid s), \qquad P(f_j \mid s) = \frac{C(f_j, s) + 1}{N_s + V}.
\end{equation}
For an HMM with start probabilities $\pi_j$, transitions $a_{ij}$ and emissions $b_j(o)$, the Viterbi solver writes every
candidate product, not just the maximum, because the comparison is the part students get wrong:
\begin{gather}
v_1(j) = \pi_j\, b_j(o_1), \qquad v_t(j) = \max_{i}\, v_{t-1}(i)\, a_{ij}\, b_j(o_t),\\
\psi_t(j) = \arg\max_{i}\, v_{t-1}(i)\, a_{ij}.
\end{gather}
For CYK with a grammar in Chomsky normal form, one step is one cell of the chart:
\begin{gather}
T[i,i] = \{A : A \to w_i\},\\
T[i,j] = \bigcup_{k=i}^{j-1}\{A : A \to B\,C,\; B \in T[i,k],\; C \in T[k{+}1,j]\}.
\end{gather}

\subsection{Model fallback}
A problem that no solver recognises may be explained by the model when a key is available. Such an explainer is marked
``model'' on the page, and it is accepted only if the text looked like a problem in the first place and the answer has a usable
shape:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:llm}
G_{\text{expl}}(x, E) = [\,\pi(x)\,] \cdot [\,3 \le |E| \le 8\,] \cdot [\,a \ne \varnothing\,] \cdot \prod_{i}[\,\mathit{title}_i \ne \varnothing\,],
\end{equation}
where $\pi(x)$ is true when $x$ contains a digit or a problem verb such as \emph{solve}, \emph{compute}, \emph{parse} or
\emph{estimate}. The gate is weaker than a computed answer, which is why the page says where the steps came from.

\section{Interactive Labs}
\label{sec:labs}
\subsection{The lab contract}
All labs share one page: presets, a view of the current state, a ``what is happening'' panel with Step, Back and Play controls,
and a ``try your own input'' form. A lab supplies only its presets, two form functions and one function that turns an input into
a list of frames:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{build}(u) = \langle f_1, \dots, f_m \rangle, \qquad f_k = (\mathit{label}_k, \mathit{title}_k, \mathit{explain}_k, \mathit{view}_k),
\end{equation}
where each frame is one small decision with its explanation and the view to draw. The last frame must be labelled
\emph{Answer}. Because the contract is this small, a hand-written lab is one function, and the same contract is what we give to
the model when it writes a lab.

\subsection{Choosing labs for a deck}
Each built-in lab declares topic phrases, and a slide gets that lab when its title or text matches one. Other slides may get a
generated lab, subject to a policy that keeps the deck usable (Table~\ref{tab:policy}). Writing $\lambda(i)$ for the lab of
slide $i$ and $\tau(i)$ for its topic, the policy is
\begin{equation}
\lambda(i) = \lambda(j) \text{ if } \tau(i) = \tau(j), \quad
|\{ i : \lambda(i) = \ell \}| \le 2 \;\forall \ell, \quad
|\{ \ell \text{ generated} \}| \le 10 .
\end{equation}

\subsection{The gate for model-written labs}
With a key, the model receives the topic and the slide text and returns JSON with a title, form fields and JavaScript. The code
will run in a student's browser, so it passes a static gate before it is used (Algorithm~\ref{alg:gate}). A lab must not reach
the network or storage, must not evaluate strings as code, must not break out of its script element, must define the four
names of the contract, and must parse.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Gate for model-written lab code}
\label{alg:gate}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require model reply $r$
\State $o \gets$ first JSON object in $r$; \textbf{if} none, or \texttt{js}, \texttt{inputs}, \texttt{title} are not strings \Return reject
\State $j \gets o.\texttt{js}$
\If{$j$ contains \texttt{</script} (any case)} \Return reject \EndIf
\If{$j$ uses \texttt{fetch}, \texttt{XMLHttpRequest}, \texttt{eval}, \texttt{document.cookie},}
 \State \hspace{1em}\texttt{localStorage} or \texttt{import(} as a whole word \Return reject
\EndIf
\If{any of \texttt{PRESETS}, \texttt{function build}, \texttt{function fill}, \texttt{function readInputs} is missing} \Return reject \EndIf
\If{\texttt{node --check} fails on stubs $+\, j$} \Return reject \EndIf
\State \Return accept
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Sanitised Diagrams}
\label{sec:svg}
Each topic slide may get a diagram drawn by the model as SVG. SVG is a document format that can carry scripts, event handlers,
embedded HTML, remote images and links, so we never insert the model's markup directly. An early version rejected any answer that
contained a disallowed element; it accepted only 71 of 206 diagrams (34.5\%), mostly because of harmless extras such as a
\texttt{style} block. The current version sanitises instead of rejecting. It parses the answer as XML, keeps only whitelisted
elements (shapes, text, groups, gradients, markers, clip paths and \texttt{use}), turns inline style declarations into
attributes, deletes event-handler attributes and \texttt{url()} references that leave the document, and rejects the diagram only
when something cannot be repaired. The acceptance condition is
\begin{multline}
G_{\text{svg}}(d) = [\,\text{parses}\,] \cdot [\,\text{no external href}\,] \cdot [\,n_{\text{shape}} \ge 5\,] \\
\cdot [\,n_{\text{text}} \le 28\,] \cdot [\,|\mathrm{PNG}(d)| \ge 400 \text{ bytes}\,],
\end{multline}
where the last factor rejects diagrams that render as an empty image. An external \texttt{href} is treated as unrepairable,
because removing it from a \texttt{use} element changes what the diagram shows.

Labels are then fitted. With font size $s$, label length $n$ characters, anchor position $x$ and canvas width $W_v$ taken from
the diagram's own \texttt{viewBox}, the estimated width and the room available are
\begin{gather}
\label{eq:fit}
w = 0.56\, s\, n, \qquad
R = \begin{cases} 2\min(x - 12,\, W_v - 12 - x) & \text{middle anchor}\\ x - 12 & \text{end anchor}\\ W_v - 12 - x & \text{start anchor}\end{cases},\\
s' = \max\!\left(11,\, s\,\frac{R}{w}\right) \quad \text{if } w > R > 0 .
\end{gather}
The accepted diagram is stored, rendered to PNG for the PowerPoint file, and served as an HTML page that the picture on the
slide links to.

\section{Grounding Filter for Titles and Explanations}
\label{sec:ground}
The model also proposes assertion titles (a full sentence that states the slide's point) and, for thin slides, a short
explanation. These are checked against the slide's own words. Let $C(t)$ be the set of words of at least four letters in $t$,
minus a short stop list. For a slide $x$, a proposed title $h$ and explanation $e$:
\begin{align}
G_{\text{title}}(x, h) &= [\,4 \le |h|_{w} \le 15\,]\cdot[\,|h|_{c} \le 100\,] \notag\\
 &\quad \cdot[\,|C(h) \cap C(x)| \ge 1\,]\cdot[\,\mathrm{first}(h) \notin \mathcal{I}\,],\\
G_{\text{expl}}(x, e) &= [\,|e|_{w} \le 100\,]\cdot[\,|C(e) \cap C(x)| \ge 2\,],
\end{align}
where $|\cdot|_w$ and $|\cdot|_c$ count words and characters and $\mathcal{I}$ is a list of 36 imperative verbs (\emph{compare},
\emph{identify}, \emph{explain}, \ldots) that would turn the title into an instruction. A title that fails keeps the source
heading; an explanation that fails is dropped. The filter is cheap and lexical, and it is not a fact checker: it catches an
answer about the wrong topic, not a subtle error on the right one.

\section{The Paced Key Pool}
\label{sec:pool}
\subsection{Pacing and throughput}
Each free-tier key allows about 15 requests per minute. We space calls on each key by $\tau = 4.2$\,s with a per-key lock, so
request $r$ on key $k$ starts at
\begin{equation}
t_r = \max\big(t_{\text{ready}},\; t_{\text{last}}(k) + \tau\big).
\end{equation}
With $k$ live keys and $w$ worker threads, each request holds a worker for at least the model latency $L$. The two limits give a
lower bound on the time for $N$ requests:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bound}
T(N, k, w) \;\ge\; N \cdot \max\!\left(\frac{\tau}{k},\; \frac{L}{w}\right).
\end{equation}
The first term is the pace limit and the second the worker limit. Neither should dominate, so we want $L/w \le \tau/k$, that is
$w \ge k\,L/\tau$. For diagram requests $L/\tau$ is about two, which is why the pool runs two requests in flight per key:
\begin{equation}
w = \max\big(4,\; \min(12,\; 2k)\big).
\end{equation}
Requests are spread round-robin: request $r$ tries the keys starting from key number $r \bmod K$.

\subsection{Spent keys}
A key that returns an authorisation error or reports its daily quota used up is recorded with the time $d(k)$, and it is not
tried again until the retry interval $\Delta = 900$\,s has passed:
\begin{equation}
\mathit{live}(t) = \{\, k : t - d(k) > \Delta \,\}.
\end{equation}
When $\mathit{live}(t)$ is empty the request goes straight to the router's other providers, and if they are unavailable too, to
the offline path of Eq.~\ref{eq:checked}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pool} summarises one request.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{One diagram request through the key pool}
\label{alg:pool}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require prompt $p$, keys $K$, counter $r$
\State $O \gets$ rotation of $K$ starting at $r \bmod |K|$; \quad $r \gets r + 1$
\For{$k \in O$ with $t - d(k) > \Delta$}
  \State wait until $t \ge t_{\text{last}}(k) + \tau$; \quad $t_{\text{last}}(k) \gets t$
  \State \textbf{try} \Return $\mathrm{generate}(k, p)$
  \State \textbf{on error} $e$: \textbf{if} $e$ is an authorisation or quota error \textbf{then} $d(k) \gets t$
\EndFor
\State \Return router fallback (Groq, then NVIDIA), or $\varnothing$ for the offline path
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Exact solvers cover a closed set of problem types, and that is a deliberate trade. Inside the set the explanation is always
right and takes under a millisecond; outside it the page says honestly that the steps came from a model, or shows nothing. We
would rather add solvers one at a time than widen the model's role.

Two of the checks found real faults in our own code: the dynamic-import gap in the lab gate and the canvas width in label fitting.
A third experiment showed that a recovery path we thought was fine wasted almost three minutes when every key was spent. None
of these appeared in normal use, which is an argument for testing a checker with inputs written to break it.

The checks have limits. The grounding filter is lexical and passes a repetitive but on-topic explanation. The lab gate is static;
a lab that passes it can still show a wrong intermediate value, although it cannot reach the network. The key-pool results use
a fixed latency, and real latency varies. Finally, we have measured correctness, coverage and time, not learning. Whether these
explainers and labs improve examination results needs a classroom study, which we plan for the next semester.

\section{Conclusion}
A lecture deck can gain worked solutions, labs, diagrams and better titles without trusting a language model blindly. Computing
what can be computed, checking what the model writes with rules that do not depend on the model, and keeping an offline path for
everything else gave us explanations that were right on every held-out problem, labs and diagrams with no unsafe content left,
and a deck that finishes even when every key is spent. A simple throughput bound told us how many requests to keep in flight,
and testing the checks with hostile inputs found faults that ordinary use had not.

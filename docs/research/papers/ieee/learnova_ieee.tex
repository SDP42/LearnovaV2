\begin{abstract}
Teachers rarely build a new slide deck for every lecture; they reuse old ones, and those decks are often hard to learn
from. Presentation generators based on large language models create new decks from a prompt, but they paraphrase the
source, drop list items and cannot be checked. We present Learnova, a system that rebuilds an existing deck instead of
generating a new one and keeps every point of the source. This paper describes the methods we designed for it:
(i) geometric rules that recover the structure of a deck exported to PDF, including a dynamic program for titles that lost
their spaces and a graphic-ratio test that decides when optical character recognition may replace a picture; (ii) an
explainable Content Decision Engine that maps each slide to one of more than twenty visual families with a confidence and
a reason; (iii) Pedagogical Slide Fitness (PSF), a slide-quality metric of the form $E^{\alpha}(1-L)^{\beta}C^{\gamma}$
derived from cognitive-load and multimedia-learning theory; (iv) CLASS, a dynamic program that splits an overloaded slide
into consecutive slides of minimum total load; (v) a glyph-measured layout method that guarantees no word is broken across
lines; and (vi) an illustration stage in which diagrams written by a language model are sanitised and validated, with
offline fallbacks and a scheduler for a pool of API keys. On 101 labelled slides the decision engine reached
96.8--97.4\% accuracy at under 1\,ms per slide; on six real decks (28--216 slides) the system kept 98--100\% of the source
facts, removed every untitled slide and produced no overflowing or broken text; CLASS never did worse than even splitting
and was strictly better on 37\% of overloaded sections.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The slides a student revises from are usually the slides the teacher has used for several years. In decks we collected
from our own semester we kept seeing the same problems: titles missing or reduced to ``Slide~9'', long lists of bullets,
equations and diagrams pasted as pictures, and nothing a student could interact with. Redesigning such a deck by hand
takes hours, so in practice it is not done.

AI presentation tools \cite{gamma,copilotppt,geminislides,canva} address a different task. They produce an attractive deck
from a prompt or a pasted document, but they rewrite the content as they go. Summarisation research has shown why this is
risky: abstractive models omit and invent content \cite{maynez2020,ji2023}. For a study aid that is not acceptable,
because the student cannot see what was lost.

Our position is that the source content should be treated as fixed and only its presentation should change. Every
structural decision---what a slide says, which visual shows it, how much goes on one slide---is made by an explicit rule
whose reason can be shown, and a language model is used only where its output can be checked against the slide. The
contributions of this paper are the methods behind that position:
\begin{enumerate}
\item rules for recovering the structure of slide PDFs, including title segmentation by dynamic programming and an OCR
acceptance test based on a graphic ratio (Section~\ref{sec:reader});
\item an explainable Content Decision Engine (Section~\ref{sec:engine});
\item the PSF metric and the CLASS segmentation algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:psf});
\item glyph-measured text fitting with a no-broken-word guarantee (Section~\ref{sec:layout});
\item sanitised AI diagrams with offline fallbacks and a key-pool scheduler (Section~\ref{sec:ill});
\item an evaluation on gold sets and real decks, reproducible from the project repository (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Recovering Slide Structure}
\label{sec:reader}
The reader works on text lines, each with a box $(x_0,y_0,x_1,y_1)$, a font size $s$ and a font. Table~\ref{tab:reader}
lists the thresholds; they were set on development decks and kept fixed for the evaluation.

\textit{Furniture.} Let $n$ be the number of pages. A line in the top 12\% or bottom 14\% of a page is furniture if its
normalised text appears in that band on at least
\begin{equation}
m(n)=\max\bigl(3,\ \lfloor 0.3\,n\rfloor\bigr)
\label{eq:furn}
\end{equation}
pages. Pictures are treated in the same way but counted only where they are drawn: some exporters list every image of a
deck in one shared resource dictionary, and counting resources would mark every figure as a logo.

\textit{Titles.} With body size $\bar s$ (the size carrying the most characters), the largest line $\ell^{*}$ in the top
third of a page of height $H$ is the title if
\begin{equation}
s(\ell^{*})\ge 1.18\,\bar s\ \ \lor\ \ \bigl(\mathrm{bold}(\ell^{*})\land y_0(\ell^{*})<0.2H\bigr).
\end{equation}
A following line continues the title if its size differs by less than 0.8\,pt, it starts within 60\,pt horizontally and its
vertical gap $g$ satisfies $-0.5\,s<g<0.9\,s$ (boxes from some exporters overlap, hence the negative bound).

\textit{Levels.} Bullet-glyph positions are clustered with tolerance $\tau=9$\,pt into anchors $a_1<\dots<a_K$, and an item
starting at $x$ has level
\begin{equation}
\ell(x)=\arg\min_{k}|x-a_k|.
\end{equation}

\textit{Glued titles.} For a capitalised token $t$ of length $L_t$ that is not a word and a vocabulary $V$ built from the
deck and a dictionary,
\begin{equation}
B(j)=\max_{\substack{i<j,\ j-i\le 18\\ t_{i:j}\in V}}\bigl(B(i)+(j-i)^2\bigr),\qquad B(0)=0,
\label{eq:dp}
\end{equation}
and the segmentation behind $B(L_t)$ is used when it has at least two words. Squaring the word length favours a few long
words over many short ones.

\textit{Picture-only slides.} If the largest picture covers at least 45\% of the page and the page has at most 12 words,
the picture is read with Tesseract \cite{smith2007}. With $I$ the dark pixels and $M$ the padded word boxes, the graphic
ratio is
\begin{equation}
\gamma=\frac{|I\setminus M|}{|I|}.
\end{equation}
The OCR text replaces the picture only if the mean confidence $\bar c\ge 62$, there are at least 12 words, $\gamma\le 0.42$,
at most 15\% of lines are symbol debris and the tokens do not look like a photographed table; otherwise the picture is
kept as a figure.

\textit{Chart debris and PPTX input.} If a slide's items average fewer than four words and at least half of their tokens
contain digits, they are the ticks of a drawn chart, and the region below the title is rendered as an image instead.
A PowerPoint file is first rendered to PDF by LibreOffice (cached by content hash) and read by the same rules.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:reader} summarises the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Reading one page of a slide PDF}
\label{alg:reader}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\mathcal{L}\gets$ text lines; drop furniture by (\ref{eq:furn}) and page counters
\State merge words on one baseline; split glued titles by (\ref{eq:dp})
\State $F\gets$ figures drawn on the page (not shared resources)
\If{$F_1$ covers $\ge 45\%$ and the page has $\le 12$ words}
  \State run OCR; \textbf{if} accepted \textbf{then} $\mathcal{L}\gets$ OCR lines \textbf{else} keep $F_1$
\EndIf
\State title $\gets$ largest top line, wrapped lines rejoined
\State items $\gets$ bullets with levels $\ell(x)$, wrapped lines rejoined
\If{items are chart debris} \State figure $\gets$ region below the title; items $\gets\varnothing$ \EndIf
\State \Return (title, items, tables, figure, kind)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Explainable Content Decision Engine}
\label{sec:engine}
For each slide a set $\mathcal{D}$ of 27 detectors is run. A detector $d$ returns nothing or a tuple
$(f_d,v_d,c_d,D_d,r_d)$: a family, a variant, a confidence $c_d\in[0,1]$, the data the renderer needs and a reason in plain
words. Tables, section outlines and reference lists are tested first because they are unambiguous. The remaining
candidates $\mathcal{C}$ pass through exclusion rules $X$ (a cycle removes a linear process; a definition removes statistic
tiles; a timeline removes cause--effect), and
\begin{equation}
d^{*}=\arg\max_{d\in X(\mathcal{C})}c_d,\qquad \hat c=\min(c_{d^{*}},\,0.97).
\end{equation}
The next three candidates are kept as alternatives for the editor. If $\mathcal{C}$ is empty, the slide becomes key-point
cards that carry every sentence in full, so choosing a visual never removes text. Card headings are made from the sentence
by removing discourse cues and pronouns, keeping the first three content words, cutting at an unbalanced parenthesis and
removing trailing function words, modals and adverbs, so a heading never ends mid-phrase (Algorithm~\ref{alg:engine}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Visual decision for one slide}
\label{alg:engine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\If{a table, section list or reference list is found} \State \Return it \EndIf
\State $\mathcal{C}\gets\{d(\mathit{slide}) : d\in\mathcal{D}\}\setminus\{\varnothing\}$; apply $X$
\If{$\mathcal{C}=\varnothing$} \State \Return key-point cards (lossless) \EndIf
\State \Return $d^{*}$, its reason and the next three alternatives
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{PSF and CLASS}
\label{sec:psf}
\subsection{Pedagogical Slide Fitness}
PSF scores a slide $s$ from three components in $[0,1]$.

\textit{Information efficiency.} With $\mathcal{N}(s)$ the concepts introduced on $s$ that did not appear on earlier slides
and a corpus-free rarity weight $\mathrm{idf}(w)\in[0.4,3.0]$ that grows with word length and is lower for common words,
\begin{equation}
U(s)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{N}(s)}\mathrm{idf}(w),\qquad
E(s)=\frac{U(s)}{U(s)+\kappa_e}\bigl(1-\rho(s)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\rho(s)$, the redundancy, is the mean Jaccard overlap of each bullet with the title or with the other bullets.

\textit{Cognitive load.} With $k$ distinct concepts, relational-cue density $r$, word count $t$, the largest visual element
count $v$ (table rows, flow nodes, cards) and a split-attention flag $\sigma_s$,
\begin{equation}
L(s)=\mathrm{sigm}\bigl(w_e\hat e+w_t\hat t+w_v\hat v+w_\sigma\sigma_s-\theta\bigr),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\hat e=\min\Bigl(1,\tfrac{k(1+r)}{e_{\mathrm{ref}}}\Bigr),\quad
\hat t=\min\Bigl(1,\tfrac{t}{t_{\mathrm{ref}}}\Bigr),\quad
\hat v=\min\Bigl(1,\tfrac{v}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}}\Bigr).
\end{equation}

\textit{Multimedia coherence.} $C(s)$ is the mean of four indicators from Mayer's principles: signalling (a real title and
a takeaway), coherence (share of sentences on the slide's topic), non-redundancy (a picture's description does not repeat
the text) and spatial contiguity (the figure is on the same slide as its text).

\textit{Combination.} The slide score is a Cobb--Douglas product, so very high load cannot be compensated by more
information:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PSF}(s)=E(s)^{\alpha}\bigl(1-L(s)\bigr)^{\beta}C(s)^{\gamma},\qquad \alpha+\beta+\gamma=1.
\end{equation}
For a deck $S=(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ the score is the geometric mean times a flow term that rewards moderate concept overlap
$o_i$ between consecutive slides:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PSF}(S)=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{PSF}(s_i)\Bigr)^{1/n}\Phi,\qquad
\Phi=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\phi(o_i),
\end{equation}
where $\phi(o)=1$ for $o\in[0.18,0.55]$, rises linearly from 0.6 below that band and falls towards 0.4 above it.
Table~\ref{tab:params} gives the default parameters.

\subsection{CLASS: load-optimal segmentation}
Given the $m$ points $b_1,\dots,b_m$ of an overloaded slide, a hard cap of $P$ points per slide and a soft target $T$, CLASS
chooses break points $0=j_0<j_1<\dots<j_K=m$ that minimise
\begin{equation}
J=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Bigl[L(G_k)+\mu\bigl(1-E(G_k)\bigr)+\nu\max\bigl(0,|G_k|-T\bigr)^2\Bigr]+\lambda K,
\end{equation}
where $G_k=(b_{j_{k-1}+1},\dots,b_{j_k})$ and $|G_k|\le P$. Because the cost of a group depends only on its own points, the
optimum follows from
\begin{equation}
F(i)=\min_{\max(0,i-P)\le p<i}\bigl[F(p)+\mathrm{cost}(b_{p+1..i})+\lambda\bigr],\qquad F(0)=0,
\end{equation}
in $O(mP)$ time (Algorithm~\ref{alg:class}). Every point is placed exactly once, so segmentation never drops content.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{CLASS}
\label{alg:class}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $F(0)\gets 0$; $F(i)\gets\infty$ for $i>0$
\For{$i=1$ to $m$}
  \For{$p=\max(0,i-P)$ to $i-1$}
    \State $c\gets F(p)+\mathrm{cost}(b_{p+1..i})+\lambda$
    \If{$c<F(i)$} \State $F(i)\gets c$; $\mathit{cut}(i)\gets p$ \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State follow $\mathit{cut}$ back from $m$ to read off the groups
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Layout Without Broken Words}
\label{sec:layout}
Lines are counted with an average advance ratio $\bar\rho$ (0.52 regular, 0.575 bold): a box $W$ inches wide holds
$\lfloor 72W/(\bar\rho s)\rfloor$ characters per line at size $s$. Single words are measured glyph by glyph,
\begin{equation}
w(u,s)=\kappa\,\frac{s}{72}\sum_{ch\in u}\rho(ch),
\end{equation}
with per-character advances $\rho$ from the font metrics and a safety factor $\kappa=1.06$; words are cut into unbreakable
pieces only after hyphens and slashes. The chosen size is
\begin{equation}
s^{*}=\max\{s\in\mathcal{G}: H(t,s,W)\le H\},\qquad \mathcal{G}=\{s_{\max},s_{\max}-0.5,\dots,s_{\min}\},
\end{equation}
with $s_{\min}=11$\,pt for body text; content that does not fit is moved to a continuation slide by CLASS instead of being
shrunk further. Each paragraph is then scaled by
\begin{equation}
\sigma=\max\Bigl(0.62,\ \min\bigl(1,\ \min_{u}\tfrac{W}{w(u,s)}\bigr)\Bigr),
\end{equation}
which guarantees that the longest word fits on one line. Wide visuals (processes, timelines, tables) keep the full slide
width; side pictures are placed only on layouts with room for them.

\section{Illustrations and Key Scheduling}
\label{sec:ill}
Every topic slide receives a picture, in this order: the source's own figure, a diagram written by a language model, and an
offline scene. The model is asked for one SVG in a $600\times720$ view box showing the mechanism of the slide's idea with the
slide's own terms. The answer is \emph{sanitised}: unsafe or unsupported elements (scripts, foreign objects, embedded images,
filters, style sheets) are removed, inline styles become attributes, any external reference rejects the diagram, and it must
contain at least five shapes and render. Labels that would cross the edge are scaled with the width estimate of
Section~\ref{sec:layout}. An accepted diagram is cached by a hash of the slide text, embedded as live SVG in the web deck,
rasterised for PowerPoint and published as a page to which the PowerPoint picture links.

Requests are spread over a pool $K$ of keys. Each key is paced on its own,
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{next}}(k)=\max\bigl(t_{\mathrm{now}},\ t_{\mathrm{last}}(k)+\Delta\bigr),\qquad \Delta=4.2\,\mathrm{s},
\end{equation}
two requests are kept in flight per key, and a key that returns an authentication or quota error is skipped for
$T_d=15$ minutes. With $|K|$ keys the sustained rate is $|K|/\Delta$ requests per second, about 1.2 for five keys
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:ill}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Illustration with fallbacks}
\label{alg:ill}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{each topic slide $s$, in parallel with $2|K|$ workers}
  \If{$s$ shows a source figure} \State use it; \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \If{$s$ has a wide visual} \State header picture (offline); \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \If{$\mathrm{hash}(s)$ is cached} \State use the cached diagram; \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \For{each live key $k$ in round-robin order}
    \State wait for the pace of $k$; $a\gets$ \Call{Ask}{$k,s$}
    \If{authentication or quota error} \State skip $k$ for $T_d$; \textbf{continue} \EndIf
    \State $g\gets$ \Call{Sanitise}{$a$}; \textbf{break}
  \EndFor
  \State attach $g$ (SVG, PNG, page) or an offline scene
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\subsection{Setup}
We used three gold sets of slide texts labelled with their acceptable visual families (development 38, held-out 32 and
blind 31; the blind set was written by a member who had not seen the rules) and six real course decks: four slide PDFs of
36, 44, 95 and 216 pages and two PowerPoint files of 28 and 63 slides, named A--F in the tables. All runs used a laptop
CPU and no API key unless stated.

dominate because the sources contain many picture slides, followed by key-point cards, outlines, prose and comparison

\subsection{PSF and CLASS}
Table~\ref{tab:psf} and Fig.~\ref{fig:psf} give the PSF components of each source deck read as-is and of its rebuilt deck.
Coherence $C$ rose on five of the six decks, because every slide gains a real title, a takeaway and a picture next to its
text. Mean PSF rose clearly on the two long PDF decks (E: 0.37\,$\to$\,0.49; F: 0.15\,$\to$\,0.24) and was unchanged on C,
but fell on A, B and D. The components show why: on those decks the rebuilt slides carry more words, since explanations,
examples and key terms are added to thin slides; this raises the estimated load $L$, and because the added sentences repeat
concepts already introduced, it lowers $E$. PSF therefore penalises exactly the enrichment we added for readability, a
calibration question we return to in Section~\ref{sec:disc}.

\subsection{Illustrations}
Without a key every topic slide receives an offline scene. With the five-key pool, ten diagrams were written and accepted in
10.1\,s in parallel. Before sanitisation replaced outright rejection, only 71 of 206 model answers (34.5\%) had been
accepted, most of them failing on inline styles and gradients; after the change, all ten diagrams of a live check passed.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:disc}
The rule-based design costs some coverage, but every decision can be read, tested and corrected, it takes under a
millisecond, and it works without a network. The PSF results show that a metric built from theory does not automatically
agree with every design choice: the explanations we add to thin slides help a reader but raise the estimated load. PSF's
weights are literature priors; calibrating them against student ratings, and a classroom study of learning outcomes, are our
next steps. OCR of photographed equations remains the weakest part of the reader, which is why such pictures are kept rather
than transcribed.

\section{Conclusion}
Learnova rebuilds the decks teachers already use rather than generating new ones. Its methods---structure recovery rules,
an explainable decision engine, the PSF metric, CLASS segmentation, glyph-measured layout and sanitised AI diagrams with
offline fallbacks---kept 98--100\% of the source facts on real decks, removed every untitled slide and never broke a word,
while running in seconds on ordinary hardware.
